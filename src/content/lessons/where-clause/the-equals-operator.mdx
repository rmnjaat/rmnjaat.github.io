Unit 1 took the index apart and showed what makes one slow. This unit turns that knowledge around: how do you recognise those problems in an SQL statement, and how do you write statements that avoid them? The where clause is the place to start.

## 1. Why the where clause comes first

The where clause states the search condition, and finding rows that match a condition is the one thing an index exists to do. That gives the where clause an outsized effect on performance. It is also the part of a statement most often written without a second thought, and a careless condition leaves the database no choice but to read a large part of the index.

<KeyIdea>A poorly written where clause is the first ingredient of a slow query.</KeyIdea>

The unit goes operator by operator: how each one affects index usage, and how to define an index so that as many queries as possible can use it. [Searching for ranges](/sql/where-clause/searching-for-ranges/), [partial indexes](/sql/where-clause/partial-and-filtered-indexes/) and [NULL](/sql/where-clause/null/) follow the material here, and the last group, [obfuscated conditions](/sql/where-clause/obfuscation/), collects the common anti-patterns and shows what to write instead.

## 2. Equality, the easy case that still goes wrong

`=` is the most trivial operator in SQL and the one used most. That does not make it safe. Indexing mistakes that cost real performance are still common around it, and where clauses that combine several conditions are the most exposed. This group of lessons shows how to confirm that an index is actually used, how a concatenated index can serve a combined condition, and, by taking a slow query apart, what the causes from unit 1 look like in practice.

[Primary Keys](/sql/where-clause/the-equals-operator/primary-keys/) looks at a lookup by primary key. The index comes with the constraint, the lookup is a unique scan, and the lesson shows how to verify that in the execution plan of each database.

[Concatenated Keys](/sql/where-clause/the-equals-operator/concatenated-keys/) adds a second column to the key. The same index becomes useless for one of the two queries, and the fix is nothing more than swapping the column order.

[Slow Indexes, Part II](/sql/where-clause/the-equals-operator/slow-indexes-part-ii/) returns to the first ingredient of a slow index. An index that covers only part of the where clause still gets used, reads far more than it needs to, and sometimes the optimizer rightly gives up on it.
